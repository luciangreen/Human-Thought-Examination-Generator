Kant argues that moral worth depends entirely on acting from duty, not
from inclination or the desire for good consequences. An action has moral
worth only if the agent acts because it is their duty, regardless of
whether the consequences are beneficial.

The categorical imperative provides the test: act only according to that
maxim by which you can at the same time will that it should become a
universal law. This requires reasoning about the universalisability of
one's intentions.

Critics argue that this framework cannot resolve conflicts between duties,
and that consequences cannot be entirely irrelevant to moral assessment.
Consequentialists claim that outcomes are the only genuine basis for
moral evaluation, making Kant's deontological position incomplete.
